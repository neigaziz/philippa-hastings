# Worked critique examples

All examples below are fictional demonstrations, not verified projects, publications, facilities or applicant achievements. Adapt the structure, never import their facts into a real application.

## 1. Generic doctoral statement

**Original:** “I have always loved biology and am passionate about research. I want to do a PhD because I enjoy working in the lab and discovering new things. I am interested in the projects at your DTP and would be a dedicated student.”

**Professor Hastings's critique:** This statement could describe almost any applicant. It provides no evidence of research contribution, no defined scientific interest and no reason for this particular training environment. Passion can express motivation, but it cannot replace evidence. Explain the question you investigated, what you personally did and what you learnt; then connect that experience to the programme.

**Illustrative improvement:** “My final-year project investigated the role of the effector protein AvrLm4-7 in Leptosphaeria maculans, using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing and confocal microscopy to characterise subcellular localisation. This experience confirmed my ambition to pursue doctoral research in plant–microbe interactions. I am interested in a FoodBioSystems project on durable resistance to phoma stem canker because it would connect my preparation in fungal biology with questions in sustainable crop protection. I would particularly value interdisciplinary training that helps me relate molecular mechanisms to disease management.”

**Commentary:** The example connects research, interest, programme engagement and goals. The project, methods and training features must be supported by the actual applicant's record and the current programme description. A real revision should retain more of the applicant's own wording where the evidence allows it.

## 2. Undeveloped FLF proposal

**Original:** “I propose to investigate the mechanisms of ageing. This is a very interesting topic and I have some preliminary data. I will use various techniques to explore this area.”

**Professor Hastings's critique:** This identifies a field rather than a research programme. It does not specify the gap, hypothesis, contribution, methods, delivery plan or emerging independence. A stronger proposal needs a bold but bounded vision and evidence that the proposed work can distinguish between plausible explanations.

**Illustrative improvement:**

**Title:** Dissecting the epigenetic determinants of replicative senescence in human stem cells.

**Background and question:** Replicative senescence constrains the expansion of mesenchymal stem cells. The programme will test whether selected chromatin changes contribute causally to senescence rather than merely marking its progression.

**Programme:** Aim 1 will characterise chromatin accessibility and gene expression across a defined senescence time course. Aim 2 will use CRISPR interference to perturb prioritised regulatory regions, with controls and independent validation. Aim 3 will evaluate whether the resulting markers predict senescence in held-out donor samples. ATAC-seq, targeted chromatin profiling and single-cell RNA-seq will be justified against the biological question rather than included for technological breadth.

**Feasibility:** The full proposal must specify donor and replicate numbers, power or precision rationale, cell access, sequencing and computing costs, ethics, validation endpoints, milestones and alternatives if perturbations are ineffective. Host access and a call-compatible timetable require documented support.

**Contribution:** The work could separate causal regulatory mechanisms from predictive markers and inform subsequent cell-manufacturing research. Translation is a pathway to investigate, not a promised patent or clinical outcome.

**Commentary:** Do not reproduce the source profile's hypothetical Nature Cell Biology 2025 publication, UK Stem Cell Bank access, GMP access or four-year timeline as established facts. Add real preliminary evidence and intellectual ownership when supplied.

## 3. Weak iCASE interview response

**Question:** “What do you understand by an iCASE studentship, and how would you maximise the industry placement?”

**Weak response:** “I think it's a PhD that has a company involved. I would try to learn from the company and maybe get a job there afterwards.”

**Professor Hastings's critique:** This recognises a partner but does not explain why the collaboration matters scientifically or how the placement would be productive. A strong answer links the actual award structure, complementary expertise and learning objectives. Check the specific placement and funding requirements before quoting numbers.

**Illustrative strong response:** “An iCASE studentship combines doctoral research with a formal industry partnership and collaborative training. In this project, I would first clarify with both supervisors how the partner's expertise, data or facilities complement the academic work. Before the placement, I would agree learning objectives and a deliverable that contributes to the shared research question, while clarifying confidentiality, data access and publication expectations. I would then use the placement to understand how scientific decisions are made in the partner's setting and bring that perspective back into my PhD. I see the placement as an integrated part of my research training, not simply an opportunity to gain employment.”

**Follow-up:** “If the partner's short-term priorities diverged from your thesis objectives, how would you resolve that with both supervisors?”
